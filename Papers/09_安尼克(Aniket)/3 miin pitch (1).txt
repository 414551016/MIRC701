
We need a Blue Team to detect and respond to Red Team activities.
And for the Blue Team to be more effective, it's better to equip it with AI capabilities so it can better recognize Red Team behavior.
However, to acknowledge advance threats Blue Team should evolve — from its first generation into later generations — with each version outperforming the previous one.
But how do we improve the Blue Team AI?
So to improve the Blue Team AI, we need to use Red Team AI — an attacker that challenges it. The Blue Team AI must train against a Red Team AI until either:
It consistently beats the Red Team so with time the Blue Team learns to detect them.
But the problem is if the Red Team doesn't improve, the Blue Team will stagnate.
Recent works either focus on one side or train both sides simultaneously — none use alternating generational co-evolution where each side must beat a stronger fixed rival to advance. This is the gap our work addresses — creating a true arms race that prevents stagnation.
We train both sides in a way that they grow together. Once we have a new generation of Blue Team, we will use it to train Red Team AI.
So with time the Red Team gets better at attacking, and the Blue Team gets better at defending.
How does this training happen?
We begin with initial versions of each:
We fix the Red Team and train the Blue Team until it meets the promotion threshold. At that point, the Blue Team graduates to its next generation. Then we fix that new Blue Team and train the Red Team until it can bypass its defenses. That Red Team then becomes the next generation.
This back-and-forth continues:
Blue trains against Red → stronger Blue.
Red trains against Blue → stronger Red.
And so on — creating a continuous arms race where both sides improve through competition.
Since cyber attacks are multi-step and sequential, the Red Agent uses Reinforcement Learning (RL) to learn how to build complete attack chains across MITRE ATT&CK tactics over time. RL helps it maximize long-term success — by chaining the right techniques from initial access to final impact.
The Blue Agent also uses RL to learn how to detect and stop these attacks at the right step. It observes synthesised logs generated by a centralised server, uses a detection component to classify malicious activity, and a response component to choose the best defensive action. RL helps the agent choose proportionate responses across episodes — enabling it to adapt over time, even as the Red Agent evolves.

First — does the Blue Team actually get better with each generation? Yes. Blue suppression rate grew from 4% at Generation 0 to 95.1% at Generation 3 under alternating co-evolution. At the same time, Red continuously discovered new attack techniques each generation — cumulative unique kill chains grew to 525 — while successful attacks required progressively deeper kill-chain steps, confirming both sides are genuinely improving through competition, not stagnating.

Second — does co-evolution actually produce stronger agents than simply training against a fixed opponent? Fixed opponent training reached high defense on paper — but that number is an illusion. It collapsed to just 2% when tested against an evolved Red agent it had never seen. Alternating co-evolution maintained 85–95% suppression across all generations even against unseen attackers. Additionally, simultaneous co-evolution came close in peak performance but suffered chronic training instability — both agents updating simultaneously made it impossible to attribute improvement to either side. Alternating co-evolution delivered better generalization, stable training, and richer behavioral diversity — with Blue developing four distinct defensive action types versus fixed training's single action over-reliance.
